01 · The starting point

Jet lag and tiredness are different

Your internal clock helps set when you feel sleepy, alert, hungry, and warm or cool. Crossing time zones moves the clock on the wall immediately. Your body clock usually takes longer to change.

You can be well rested and still struggle to sleep at a new local bedtime. You can also feel exhausted after a long flight without crossing any time zones. Rephase considers both the clock shift and the opportunity to get enough sleep. [1]

☾

The aim: help sleep and wakefulness happen at useful local times, while protecting your rest along the way.

02 · The strongest timing signal

Light tells your clock what time it is

Light reaching your eyes is a powerful signal to the body clock. The same light can move the clock earlier or later, depending on when your body receives it. That is why “get sunlight when you land” can be too simple. [2]

A broad pattern seen in controlled light experiments
When your body thinks it isTypical effect of bright light
Late biological evening or night, before the daily body-temperature lowMoves the clock later
After that daily low, toward biological morningMoves the clock earlier

“Biological morning” means morning according to your internal clock. It can differ greatly from the destination's local morning. As you adjust, the useful light windows move too.

Rephase suggests light and dim-light windows using an estimate of this internal timing. Airport coordinates help check whether outdoor daylight is available; after sunset, the plan labels a bright indoor-light option instead. Weather, buildings, and your actual exposure remain unknown.

Seek outdoor daylight when practical, without looking at the sun. For a dim-light window, reduce bright indoor light and screens, or use an eye mask when resting. Always keep enough visibility for safe travel. Cabin lighting and your actual light exposure can change the effect. [1]

03 · Make room for real rest

A sleep window is an opportunity

Your usual sleep and wake times give the planner a starting point. Your preferred destination times and sleep duration describe what you want to work toward. Chronotype adds context: some people naturally prefer earlier mornings, while others prefer later evenings.

Those preferences help estimate timing, but they do not measure your circadian phase. The research model does not assign an invented clock offset to a chronotype label. Even regular sleepers can have different internal timing. A laboratory assessment of melatonin onset under dim light is more informative than a questionnaire. [4]

On flights, the plan excludes the first 30 minutes after takeoff and the last 45–60 minutes before landing from sleep opportunities. The default landing buffer is 60 minutes. These are practical cabin constraints, rather than a research-derived treatment. Crew instructions and actual conditions take priority.

Travel class and whether you have a lie-flat bed help make those opportunities more realistic. A bed can make rest easier; it does not guarantee sleep or a faster clock shift. Connections, transfers, and upright seating can reduce the rest you actually get. If a long sleep window is not feasible, the plan should still protect realistic rest and avoid treating time in a seat as hours slept.

✦

Keep it achievable. Research on travelers shows that people often cannot follow mathematically ideal schedules. A workable plan matters. [5]

Meals · Protect your rest

Let sleep set the meal-service plan

The CDC recommends using destination time for meals, sleep, and light where practical, and warns that sleep loss can worsen jet lag. We protect the sleep opportunities in your flight plan: if a flight includes planned rest, ask the crew to let you sleep through meal service. Eat while awake before boarding, after resting, or after arrival instead. [1]

Each flight shows a meal-service recommendation and suggested eating windows in both airport clocks. These fit around planned sleep, taxi and takeoff/landing restrictions, and nearby connections. Airline service times and food availability are unknown. A suggested window is flexible; it is not a reason to go hungry or to interrupt needed sleep. Meal calendar entries have no alarms.

A light snack can be more comfortable close to sleep than a heavy meal. The U.S. National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute recommends avoiding large meals within a few hours of bedtime and says a light snack is fine. [12]

Food timing can affect metabolic clocks. A small human experiment shifted glucose rhythms without shifting melatonin or cortisol rhythms; a separate simulated night-work study found benefits for glucose regulation from daytime eating. These were not trials showing faster jet-lag recovery in travelers. We do not prescribe fasting or claim that precisely timed meals reset your main body clock. Food effects are not part of the light-driven model. [13] [14]

04 · An optional timing signal

Melatonin: timing matters more than “more”

Your body normally produces melatonin at night. A supplement can signal darkness to the clock and can make you sleepy. Its timing matters: taking it at the wrong biological time can move the clock in an unhelpful direction. Studies comparing 0.5 mg and 3 mg found that the timing of the strongest response also differed between doses. [3]

The CDC says 0.5–1 mg is often sufficient to produce a circadian shift. Rephase shows this as general adult dose information when an optional window is included. It is not an individually calculated prescription. A larger time-zone change does not mean you should take a larger dose. [1]

Melatonin is optional and off by default. The research planner shows general evening information only for selected earlier-adjustment schedules with a full ground sleep opportunity and compatible estimated biological timing. It omits these windows during flights, transfers, preparation, and later-adjustment schedules. This conservative rule cannot determine your optimal individual timing. Never wake solely to take it. Do not take it before driving or an activity that needs alertness.

Check suitability with a clinician or pharmacist if you take medicines or have a medical condition. Seek specific advice for pregnancy or breastfeeding, children, epilepsy, blood-thinning medicines, or older adults with dementia. Daytime drowsiness, interactions, and inconsistent supplement contents are documented concerns. Long-term safety is less well established. [7]

05 · Alertness without borrowing from tonight

Caffeine helps you feel awake

Caffeine can support alertness, but it does not replace sleep. It can also interfere with your next sleep period, including when taken several hours before bedtime. A controlled study found disrupted sleep even when a substantial dose was taken six hours before bed. [8]

Rephase uses an eight-hour cutoff before a planned sleep window as a conservative planning choice. This is not a precise personal clearance time: effects vary with dose, sensitivity, medicines, and how quickly you metabolize caffeine. The CDC advises stopping at least six hours before bedtime, and a broader review also found that earlier cutoffs can protect sleep. [1] [9]

Enable caffeine only if you want it in your plan. Use your usual tolerance as a guide, and skip it if it makes you anxious, unwell, or unable to sleep.

06 · Every stop is connected

A round trip is more than two one-way trips

Your clock may still be adjusting when the next flight begins. A multi-stop plan therefore needs to carry its estimated clock state from one destination to the next, rather than reset you to “fully adjusted” at each airport.

For a short stay, complete adjustment may not be useful. Keeping closer to home time or aiming for partial adjustment can make sense, depending on your commitments and where you go next. The CDC includes maintaining home sleep hours as an option for short trips. [1]

A 2026 study simulated more than 55,000 flights and found that departure and arrival times, in-flight sleep, and light around travel changed the modeled duration of jet lag. For some time-zone changes, the itinerary could even change the direction in which the clock adjusted. [6]

This supports planning around the whole itinerary. It is a computer modeling study at a group-average level, not a clinical trial showing that Rephase improves travelers' outcomes.

07 · Read the plan with context

Useful guidance, with honest uncertainty

Rephase includes an experimental planner based on Forger99, a published mathematical model of the light-driven human body clock. It compares achievable earlier- and later-adjustment schedules across the itinerary, then checks the selected schedule under different starting-phase assumptions. [10]

The implementation uses an attributed, open-source reference. Its equations and numerical trajectories have been compared with independent reference calculations for a set of test scenarios. This verifies that the software calculates those model equations consistently. It does not show that the model predicts your clock accurately or that the app reduces real-world jet lag. [11]

The model assumes light levels and habitual sleep timing. A sleep opportunity represents darkness; it does not calculate sleep debt or sleep quality. Caffeine and melatonin guidance is separate general information: their biological effects are not simulated. Completion logs can update assumed past light or darkness, but do not measure your actual exposure or circadian phase.

The plan's body-clock change compares successive simulated daily low points. It is not a measured change in your body. A date with too little modeled history shows an unavailable estimate. When a travel date includes multiple timezones, it has one date tile and uses one labeled timezone's estimate rather than adding overlapping estimates together.

A sensitivity check shifts the starting-phase assumption by two hours in either direction. Those alternative simulations illustrate uncertainty; they are not a medical confidence interval or a guarantee that your actual timing falls inside that range.

Rephase remains an evidence-informed prototype. It does not directly measure your body clock, know how well you slept, or provide medical diagnosis or treatment. Clinical validation and individual prediction accuracy remain separate work.

Rephase is independently developed and is not affiliated with Timeshifter. It does not reproduce or claim equivalence to that app's proprietary algorithm.

Persistent sleep problems, significant daytime impairment, or a medical question deserve advice from a qualified healthcare professional. Your safety and actual sleep needs take priority over any schedule.

Trusted sources & further reading

Clinical guidance, public health information, and original research. Each description explains what that source supports.

  1. CDC Yellow Book 2026 · Jet Lag Disorder ↗

    A practical clinical overview of timed light, sleep, short trips, caffeine, and melatonin.

  2. Khalsa et al., 2003 · A phase response curve to single bright light pulses in human subjects ↗

    Controlled laboratory evidence that bright light has different clock-shifting effects at different biological times.

  3. Burgess et al., 2010 · Human phase response curves to daily melatonin: 0.5 mg versus 3.0 mg ↗

    Laboratory comparison of dose-dependent melatonin timing; not a personal dosing calculator.

  4. Reiter et al., 2020 · Finding DLMO: estimating dim light melatonin onset from sleep markers ↗

    Why habitual sleep times, diaries, and questionnaires are imperfect substitutes for measuring circadian phase.

  5. Christensen et al., 2020 · Optimal adjustment of the human circadian clock in the real world ↗

    Traveler observations and modeling that highlight the importance of achievable schedules and adherence.

  6. Huang et al., 2026 · Modeling the effects of flight itinerary on jetlag duration ↗

    Group-average simulations of flight timing, light, and in-flight sleep. Journal version (DOI).

  7. NCCIH · Melatonin: What You Need To Know ↗

    U.S. National Institutes of Health information on safety, interactions, product variability, and populations needing specific advice.

  8. Drake et al., 2013 · Caffeine effects on sleep taken 0, 3, or 6 hours before going to bed ↗

    A controlled study showing that a substantial caffeine dose can disrupt sleep even six hours before bedtime.

  9. Gardiner et al., 2023 · The effect of caffeine on subsequent sleep: a systematic review and meta-analysis ↗

    A synthesis of sleep studies; caffeine dose and timing both influence sleep disruption.

  10. Forger, Jewett & Kronauer, 1999 · A simpler model of the human circadian pacemaker ↗

    The original publication behind the experimental light-driven oscillator. A population model is not a measurement of an individual's clock.

  11. Arcascope circadian · Pinned open-source model reference ↗

    The MIT-licensed Forger99 reference used for the port and numerical comparisons. Reference license and attribution.

  12. NHLBI · Healthy Sleep Habits ↗

    Public-health guidance on protecting sleep, avoiding heavy meals near bedtime, and choosing a light snack if needed.

  13. Wehrens et al., 2017 · Meal Timing Regulates the Human Circadian System ↗

    A small laboratory study of metabolic and peripheral rhythms. It did not establish a traveler jet-lag treatment or a shift in melatonin/cortisol timing.

  14. Chellappa et al., 2021 · Daytime eating prevents internal circadian misalignment and glucose intolerance in night work ↗

    A small randomized simulated-night-work experiment, relevant to metabolic timing rather than proof of jet-lag recovery. NIH plain-language summary.
